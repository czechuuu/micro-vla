\section*{Opening Remarks}
Throughout the report, the model that we are discussing is not a VLA.
Initially we experimented with a pretrained, 500M parameter VLA - \href{https://huggingface.co/lerobot/smolvla\_base}{\textit{SmolVLA}}, however we quickly realized that it wasn't computationally feasible for us.
One epoch of fine-tuning the action head (not even the VLM backbone) took around an hour on a Google Colab T4 GPU, and yielded around 10\% success rate on the simplest task we were interested in.
We decided to train an order of magnitude smaller decision transformer from scratch (whose architecture will be disclosed in a later section).

\section{The Environments}
We decided to use \href{https://robosuite.ai/}{\textit{robosuite}}, which its authors describe as "a simulation framework powered by the MuJoCo physics engine for robot learning (...) (that) also offers a suite of benchmark environments for reproducible research".
The decision was motivated by its easily configurable environments with an option to swap out robot arms to any of the available models.

\subsection{Tasks}
We chose a subset of three tasks, based on the following criteria: they have an obvious difficulty ordering, they can be seen as a natural extension of one another, and they have accompanying dataset (with an asterisk described in a later section).

\subsection{Robot Arms}
Throughout the project we use two robot arms: Panda and IIWA.
The first one was chosen, because it was present in the datasets that we had access to (described later).
The second one was selected for its similarity to the Panda - 6 degrees of freedom with similar kinematics and a single-degree-of-freedom gripper.
Two distinct arms were necessary to test cross-embodiment generalization.

We've decided to use the related Robomimic dataset to train our models. 
The first task we wanted to achieve is to train a model on a task (e.g. Lift - which consists of picking up a red cube from the table), and evaluate it on a slight variation of the same task (e.g. changing the colour of the cube to blue, or changing the shape to a cylinder). 
The second task we wanted to achieve is to train a model on a set of tasks (e.g. Lift, and Nut Assembly - which consists of putting a nut on top of a bolt), and evaluate it on a more complex task (e.g. Stack - which consists of picking up a red cube from the table, and stacking it on top of a blue cube).

We had to postprocess the robomimic PH dataset as it wasn't publicly released in a full form with the images included and dense rewards.

We had also built an evaluation suite - an extendable framework for evaluating models on the Robosuite environment. It allows us to easily add new tasks, abstract away the implementation details of the environment, and of the models.
\section{Initial Idea - finetuning a pretrained VLA model}
Since the project had VLA in its name our initial idea was to start from a pretrained VLA model and to fine-tune it on our tasks. 
We have decided to use one called SmolVLA mainly because of it's low computational requirements - most VLA would be out of our reach to fine tune on a single consumer grade (i.e. colab T4 with 14GB VRAM GPU).
We have spent some time trying to get it to work - and have managed to get some first successful attempts (although very rare - success rate was approx. 10\%) on the Lift task, but we have realized that with one epoch taking over 1h of compute this approach wasn't going to work well. 

\section{Pivot - traning a Decision Transformer from (almost) scratch}
In order to be able to iterate faster and test more ideas we have decided to pivot and to train a Decision Transformer from scratch - but using a pretrained vision encoder (e.g. Dino).
This combined with preprocessing the dataset to contain Dino embeddings of every frame allowed us to train a model in a matter of minutes instead of hours.

\subsection{In-context learning}
We also implemented in-context learning - where we also provide the model with a successful trajectory of the task (also during training).
To get better insights into it we first evaluated it on the Lift task.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering % Centers the image horizontally

    \caption{Performance of the model trained with in-context learning on the Lift task.}
    \label{fig:baseline_icl}

Since it still didn't show any performance improvements we have also tried to use time based rewards - where we give a reward of -1 at every step, and +100 on success. This is meant to help the model discriminate between better and worse trajectories (e.g. ones that are closer to success, or ones that achieve success faster).

\subsection{Training on multiple tasks}
Since any of the above approaches still couldn't give us success on the Stack task we have decided to try to train a model on the Lift and Nut Assembly tasks and evaluate it on the Stack task.
The motivation was that it would force the model to learn that there is more than one task - and to actually look at the successful demonstration in the context.
This still didn't give us any improvements on the Stack performance. But upon inspecting the trajectories we have realized the cause - the model was drastically overfitting. 
The model (trained on both Nut and Lift) when tasked with Lift during evaluation would always reach in the direction of the non-existent Nut - and wouldn't reach for the cube.
Upon closer inspection of our prior results from the Lift task we have realized that the model was overfitting there as well - but it didn't show up as drastically due to the nature of the environment.
The failures it had were often (but not always) due to the cube being towards the edge of its spawning position - and the model would then reach not in its direction but rather towards the center of the spawn position.  

We'd tried to use weighted data sampling to mitigate this - in hopes that when we gave a higher weight to Lift the model would still remember how to reach for the cube but the smaller weight on Nut Assembly would help it learn to genralize to new tasks but with no success.

\subsection{Trying to stack cubes anyway}
We were determined to stack the cubes though so we have decided to try to train a model on the Lift task (PH dataset of 200 trajectories) and a small manually collected dataset of 10 trajectories of Stack.
We'd also decided to use weighted data sampling to give a higher weight to the Stack task - in hopes that it would help the model learn to stack the cubes.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering % Centers the image horizontally
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/plots/lift_and_stack.png}
    \caption{Performance of the model trained on Lift and Stack tasks.}
    \label{fig:lift_and_stack}
\end{figure}
This showed some results - we were able to sometimes (altough rather rarely) stack the cubes while still retaining decent performance on the Lift task. 
This is a nice result because training the Decision Transformer on only the 10 Stack trajectories was FAR from enough to learn to stack the cubes. 
But providing a solid foundation of the Lift task and then giving it a small amount of data on the Stack task was enough to get some successful stacking trajectories.
